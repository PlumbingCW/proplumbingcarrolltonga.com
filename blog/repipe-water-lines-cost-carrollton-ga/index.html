Repipe Water Lines Cost: A Carrollton, GA Price Guide

Repiping replaces the water supply lines in a house, and the price depends on the size of the house, the pipe you choose, and how hard the lines are to reach. We do not publish a single number because the range is wide. This guide explains what moves the cost and how to compare quotes.

By the Carrollton Pro Plumbing team

Call (770) 214-4545

The main factors behind a repipe price

Start with the house. A single-story home with two bathrooms takes less pipe and less time than a three-story house with four. The number of fixtures matters more than the square footage, because each fixture needs a branch.

Then the pipe. PEX costs less in material and takes less time to run, since it bends around corners and threads through walls. Copper costs more in material and labor because each joint is soldered. Mixed systems use copper where it is exposed and PEX in the walls.

Everything that moves the total

These are the items a good quote itemizes.

  • Number of fixtures, including outdoor spigots, an ice maker line, and washing machine boxes.
  • Pipe material and fittings.
  • Access: a crawlspace is easier than a slab, and an attic route is easier than finished walls.
  • Number of stories and the layout of the house.
  • Whether drywall repair is included or left for your contractor.
  • Permit and inspection costs where they apply.
  • Whether the water heater connections and shutoff valves are replaced at the same time.

Repair versus repipe: the cost question

The decision often comes down to how many leaks you have had.

Situation.Better choice.Why.
One leak in an otherwise sound system.Repair.A repair fixes the one failure.
Two or three leaks in different places over a few years.Price a repipe.The pipe wall is failing throughout.
Rusty water, low pressure, galvanized pipe.Repipe.The pipe is clogged inside and corroding.
Planned remodel opening the walls anyway.Repipe at the same time.The walls are open and labor drops.

How to compare repipe quotes

Ask for the pipe type, the number of fixtures, whether drywall is included, and what happens if hidden damage is found. A written quote that lists those items lets you compare line by line. A low total with no detail often leaves out drywall or a permit.

Ask how long the water will be off. A good plan keeps the shutdown to a day or two, with water restored in the evening. Ask also what warranty comes with the work, and get it in writing.

Saving money without cutting corners

Combine the repipe with other work behind the walls, such as a bathroom remodel or electrical updates. Open walls are expensive to open and cheap to leave open. Repiping during a remodel is usually cheaper than repiping alone and then remodeling.

Do not skip the pressure test. A repipe that is not tested before the walls close can hide a bad joint. We test every system before we close up. See plumbing installation for how we plan remodel work.

What a repipe visit involves

The plumber maps the house, shuts the water off, and opens small access holes in drywall at key points. New lines run through walls, attic, or crawlspace, and every fixture is reconnected. Then the walls are patched or the patching is handed to a drywall trade.

Plan for the water to be off for part of one or more days. Ask how long the shutoff will last, where the plumber will stage tools, and who patches and paints. A written scope removes surprises.

PEX or copper: how to think about it

PEX is flexible, goes in faster, and handles freezing better than rigid pipe. Copper is durable and familiar, and some buyers prefer it. Both are accepted for home supply lines when installed to code. The better choice depends on access, budget, and how the house is built.

Ask which fittings the plumber uses, how the lines are supported, and what the warranty covers. A plumber who explains the choice clearly is easier to trust than one who pushes a single product.

Carrollton, GA note: older homes and lead

The City of Carrollton is developing a water service line inventory to identify areas where lead and galvanized iron lines may exist, and it asks owners of structures built in 1989 or earlier to check. Its page states that the utility-owned portion of the system has no known lead pipes. The line from the meter to your house is private, and so are the pipes inside.

The city's water quality report states that its lead and copper results meet the EPA standard, and says these metals mostly enter water from corrosion of household plumbing. The EPA says lead pipes are more likely in homes built before 1986. If your house is older and has galvanized or unknown pipe, a repipe is worth pricing.
