Water line guide

How to Check Your Water Line for Lead or Galvanized Pipe in Carrollton, GA

The City of Carrollton is building an inventory of water service lines. It asks owners of structures built in 1989 or earlier to check what their line is made of. The test takes a few minutes, a magnet, and a key. This guide walks through it, explains what each result means, and covers what to do if you find a galvanized or lead line.

By the Carrollton Pro Plumbing team

Call (770) 214-4545

What the city is doing and why it matters

On its service line identification page, the City of Carrollton says it is building an inventory. The goal is to find where lead and galvanized iron lines may exist. It says the utility-owned portion of the system has no known lead pipes, and it asks customers who own structures built in 1989 or earlier to check their own service line, the section between the meter and the building.

The inventory helps the city plan, and it helps you. Knowing your line material tells you whether to expect corrosion, low pressure, or leaks, and whether to flush your taps before drinking after the water has sat. Even customers who find no lead are asked to submit results.

The scratch and magnet test

These are the common home checks for identifying a service line. You need a magnet, a key or coin, and a flashlight.

  1. Find the line.

    Look where the water line enters the building, usually near the main shutoff valve in a basement, crawlspace, or utility room.

  2. Scratch lightly.

    Use a key to scratch a small spot through any corrosion. Wash your hands afterward and do not scrape hard.

  3. Try the magnet.

    A magnet sticks to galvanized steel. It does not stick to lead or copper.

  4. Read the color and sound.

    A shiny silver scratch with a dull tap sounds like lead. A copper scratch with a ringing tap is copper. A dull gray scratch with a magnet that sticks is galvanized.

What each result means

The same tests separate the three common metals.

Scratch is shiny silver, magnet does not stick, dull tap.Lead.Contact the utility and consider replacing the line, see repiping.
Scratch is copper-colored, magnet does not stick, ringing tap.Copper.Generally sound. Watch for pinhole leaks in older lines.

If you find galvanized steel

Galvanized steel is zinc-coated iron pipe. The coating wears away over decades and the steel corrodes from the inside. Rust narrows the pipe, which lowers pressure, and it can flake into the water. Threaded joints are the usual leak points.

Galvanized pipe is not an emergency by itself. If the pressure is fine and there are no leaks, you can plan the work. If the water runs brown after sitting, the pressure is low, or leaks have started, replacing the line is worth pricing. Our leak detection service can find the weak spots first.

If you find lead

The EPA says lead gets into drinking water when plumbing corrodes, and that lead pipes, faucets, and fixtures are the most common sources. It recommends flushing taps after water has sat and using a filter certified to remove lead.

If your line is lead, tell the utility and talk to a licensed plumber about replacement. Until it is replaced, run the tap before drinking or cooking after water has sat, and use a certified filter. Replacing the private portion of the line is a plumbing project that the city can advise you on.

Other places old pipe hides

The service line is only one place to look. Older homes often have galvanized or lead-soldered pipe inside the walls too, and the line entering the house can be a different material from the branches. Look at exposed pipe in the basement, crawlspace, or under sinks, and note whether it is gray steel, copper, or plastic.

If you see mixed materials, you likely have a house that was repiped in part. A dark greenish crust at joints points to copper that is corroding. Rust stains at threaded joints point to galvanized steel. Take photos so a plumber can see what you saw.

Flushing and filtering while you decide

While you plan, run the cold tap for a minute or two before drinking if the water has sat for hours, such as first thing in the morning. Use cold water for drinking and cooking, since hot water pulls more metal from pipes. A filter certified for lead removal adds a second layer.

Make a note of the date, what you found, and photos of the pipe. If you sell the house or file with the utility, that record saves time.

Carrollton, GA note: what the city's water report says

The City of Carrollton's annual water quality report states that its lead and copper results meet EPA standards. It lists the source of these metals as corrosion of household plumbing, which is the reason the city asks owners of older homes to look at the pipe inside their own property.

For help with the inventory, use the contact details on the city's service line identification page, or call the water billing office at 770-830-2000.
