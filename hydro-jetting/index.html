What hydro jetting actually does

A jetting machine feeds pressurized water through a flexible hose with a nozzle that fires jets forward and backward. The forward jets break the blockage. The rear jets pull the hose through the pipe and blast grease and scale off the pipe wall as it goes.

The result is a line cleaned to the pipe wall instead of a narrow channel cut through the middle. That matters for grease, which cools into a coat on the inside of a drain and shrinks the opening a little more with every use.

Backup emergency: when jetting cannot wait

A restaurant kitchen that cannot drain loses a service night. A house where every drain is backing up has sewage on the floor. In both cases call the emergency line, stop running water, and tell us what is backing up and where the cleanout sits.

If a plumber has already cabled the line twice this season and it blocked again, say so. That pattern tells us a cable is not enough and we bring the jetter first.

Jetting, cabling, or a camera first

The camera decides whether jetting is safe. A pipe that is cracked or collapsed can be damaged by pressure.

What jetting clears well

These are the situations where it earns its place.

  • Kitchen drain lines coated with cooking grease.
  • Main sewer lines narrowed by years of sludge.
  • Roots that have been cut back by a cable and need the debris flushed.
  • Scale buildup in older cast iron and galvanized lines.
  • Floor drains and grease interceptors in commercial kitchens.
  • Storm drains that collect silt and leaves.

How we jet a line safely

Every job follows the same order.

  1. Camera the line.

    We look first so we know the pipe is sound and where the problem sits.

  2. Match the nozzle.

    A rooting nozzle, a grease nozzle, and a scale nozzle do different things. We pick one for what we saw.

  3. Work the length.

    We jet forward to the blockage, then pull back to clean the wall.

  4. Confirm with a second look.

    We run the camera again to show the cleaned pipe and any defects the debris hid.

Local conditions that make jetting worthwhile

Carrollton is home to a university, restaurants around Adamson Square, and commercial strips along US Highway 27. Commercial kitchens in particular put grease into lines every day, and a jetting schedule is cheaper than an emergency call after each stoppage. Tree roots around older sewer laterals are the other regular cause. Roots grow back, but jetting after a cable pass removes the debris they catch and buys a longer clear period.

Code and permits for this work

Georgia's Department of Community Affairs publishes the state's amendments to the International Plumbing Code (IPC), the standard that plumbing work in Georgia is measured against, and the City of Carrollton issues plumbing permits through its permits office. Jetting is maintenance and does not need a permit, but any line replaced after a camera run is plumbing work the code covers.

What changes the price of a jetting visit

Jetting costs more than a hand cable because the equipment is larger, so we explain the reason for each job.

  • Line size and length from the access point.
  • How heavy the grease or scale buildup is.
  • Whether a cleanout is available or we have to pull a toilet.
  • Camera inspection before and after.
  • After-hours or weekend dispatch.

We quote before we start and tell you if we think a cable pass is enough. Insurance often does not cover cleaning a blockage, so check your policy.
